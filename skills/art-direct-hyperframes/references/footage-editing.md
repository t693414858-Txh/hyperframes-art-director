# Footage selection and editorial rhythm

Use for case analysis, documentary and other footage-led work. Consult the
project's confirmed style preferences first. Related people, meaningful actions
and work environments can carry an analysis while evidence appears when needed.

## Select shots by function

Plan what each shot lets the viewer understand: establish a place, observe an
action, inspect a detail, see a reaction, compare results, read evidence or resolve
a question. Choose wide, medium, close and reverse views where the material
supports continuity. Do not impose a fixed shot count or universal footage ratio.

Assess variety across work stages, actions, environments and shot scales.
Additional faces or files alone do not demonstrate variety. A single attractive
location should not represent unrelated steps throughout the film. A scene that
does not illustrate the nearby narration is a sourcing problem, not a transition
problem.

Keep a shot ledger beside the storyboard, using the project's existing format:

| Field | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Shot ID and timeline in/out | Locate the exact use in the current edit |
| Source ID and source in/out | Detect overlapping reuse of source footage |
| Work stage, action, environment, shot scale | Inspect actual visual variety |
| Narrative purpose and nearby claim | Explain why this shot belongs here |
| Evidence role | Direct evidence, illustrative footage, reconstruction or generated concept |
| Source URL, license and local path | Trace the material and its permitted use |
| Crop and continuity notes | Check faces, proportions, screen direction and action continuity |
| Reuse reason | Identify an intentional callback, replay or freeze |

## Inspect source repetition

Compare source IDs and time intervals, not filenames of exported crops. Two cuts
of the same source overlap when `max(in_a, in_b) < min(out_a, out_b)`.
Different zooms, color grades, mirrored copies or filenames do not make those
frames new material. Record speed changes when mapping timeline time to source time.

Flag repeated intervals for replacement unless the repeat has a stated editorial
purpose. A closing freeze may recall an earlier shot, but must be marked as a
callback and excluded from claims about new footage. Non-overlapping extracts
from one source still need visual variety review: they may show the same action
and setting repeatedly.

## Make rhythm serve the argument

Use shorter shots to establish the situation, hold longer for an important action
or readable evidence, and change pace at a change of thought. Cut on action or a
semantic beat when appropriate. Review the sequence with narration and sound;
equal durations, a high cut count or continuous movement do not prove good rhythm.

Keep source footage movement distinct from added camera motion. Preserve natural
direction and focal continuity across adjacent shots. Reframing should direct
attention to a specific detail. Use transitions for a change of place, time or
argument; repeated elaborate transitions weaken continuity.

## Review the current edit

Inspect the rendered film and ledger together. Add failures to the existing
scorecard's `blocking_issues`; do not invent a second aesthetic scoring system.

- Does each scene advance the argument through an action, result or source?
- Are work stages and environments varied where the story changes?
- Are repeated source intervals documented and justified?
- Can the viewer read evidence before it disappears?
- Do cuts preserve identity, proportions and action continuity?
- Are illustrative people and images clearly distinguished from actual evidence?
- Does the pacing preserve the approved style while addressing the user's critique?

Record exact timestamps and a correction for each failure. A ledger check can
establish interval reuse; it cannot establish that the film is engaging. Playback
review and user feedback remain necessary evidence for aesthetic judgments.
